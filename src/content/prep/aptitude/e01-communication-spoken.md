=== c-reading-aloud | Reading Aloud
summary: Reading a passage clearly at a natural pace, pausing at punctuation and stressing key words, with recorded practice.
---
Many hiring assessments (Versant, AMCAT/SVAR, campus spoken-English rounds) begin with a reading-aloud task. You are scored on **pronunciation, fluency and pace**, not on accent.

## What evaluators listen for

- **Pace:** about **120–160 words per minute**. Rushing hurts clarity; reading too slowly sounds hesitant.
- **Pauses:** a short pause at commas, a full stop at periods. Don't pause in the middle of a phrase.
- **Chunking:** read in meaningful groups: *The new policy / will take effect / from next month.*
- **Stress:** lean slightly on the key content words (nouns, main verbs, numbers).
- **Clarity:** pronounce word endings, such as the -s, -ed and -t in *asked*, *results* and *tests*.

## Practice routine

1. Listen to the model reading.
2. Read silently once and mark the pauses in your head.
3. Record yourself and check the words-matched score and your pace.
4. Re-read, fixing any words shown in red.

:::speak read
Good communication is not about using difficult words. It is about expressing your ideas clearly, so that the listener understands you the first time.
:::

:::speak read
Our team completed the project two weeks ahead of schedule. The client was pleased with the results and has asked us to begin the second phase in March.
:::

:::speak read
Before you submit the report, please check the figures in the third table carefully, and make sure that every chart has a clear title.
:::

=== c-listening | Listening Comprehension
summary: Listening for the gist and the details, note-taking, and answering questions about a spoken passage.
---
Listening tests (in Versant, SVAR, and interviews themselves) check whether you can **catch the main idea and specific details on a single hearing**.

## Strategies

- **Before listening:** read the questions, so you know what to listen for.
- **While listening:** note **keywords, numbers, names and times**, not full sentences.
- **Listen for signposts:** *first*, *however*, *the main reason*, *in conclusion*. They flag the important points.
- **After listening:** answer from your notes. Don't choose an option just because it repeats a word you heard.

Practise with the transcript hidden: play the audio once, answer the questions, then reveal the transcript to check.

Attendance is compulsory for all new joiners. Laptops will be provided, so you do not need to bring your own. Also, the cafeteria will close early on Friday, at 2 pm, for maintenance. Finally, please submit your travel claims for last month by the 10th, through the online portal, not by email.
:::

=== c-sentence-repetition | Sentence Repetition
summary: Hearing a sentence once and repeating it exactly, using chunking and short-term memory techniques.
---
In sentence repetition (a core Versant/SVAR task), you **hear a sentence once** and must **repeat it word for word**. It measures listening, memory and pronunciation together.

## Techniques

- **Chunk the sentence** into 2–4 meaning groups as you hear it: *Please remind me / to call the client / after lunch.*
- **Focus on meaning**, not individual words. Meaning is easier to remember, and the words follow from it.
- **Start speaking promptly.** A long delay lets the memory fade.
- **Keep the function words** (to, the, after); dropping them counts as an error.
- If you lose a word, keep the rhythm and finish. A complete sentence with one slip beats stopping.

:::speak repeat
Please remind me to call the client after lunch.
:::

:::speak repeat
The meeting has been postponed until next Wednesday morning.
:::

:::speak repeat
Could you send me the updated report before the end of the day?
:::

Q: What is the best way to remember a sentence you hear only once?
- Memorise each word separately
- [x] Group it into meaningful chunks
- Write it down
- Focus on the first word only
> Chunks reduce the load on your memory.
:::

=== c-fluency | Fluency
summary: Speaking smoothly without long pauses or fillers: preparation habits, linking phrases and timed practice.
---
**Fluency** means speaking smoothly, at an even pace, without long pauses, false starts or constant fillers ("um", "like", "you know"). It is different from **accuracy** (correct grammar); interview speaking needs both.

## Building fluency

- **Use linking phrases** to buy thinking time naturally: *That's an interesting question…*, *Let me think about that…*, *The main reason is…*
- **Replace fillers with a short silent pause.** A one-second pause sounds confident; "umm" doesn't.
- **Speak in complete thoughts.** Plan one idea, say it, then plan the next.
- **Daily practice:** speak for 1 minute on a random topic and record it. Count your fillers and aim to reduce them.

:::speak free
Talk about a skill you have learned recently. How did you learn it, and how will it help you in your career?
:::

:::speak free
Describe your ideal workplace. What kind of team, culture and work would make you do your best?
:::

Q: What does fluency mean?
- Using only advanced vocabulary
- [x] Speaking smoothly without long pauses or constant fillers
- Speaking very fast
- Having a native accent
> Fluency is about flow, not speed or accent.
:::

- [x] A brief silent pause, or "Let me think about that…"
- "You know…"

=== c-active-listening | Active Listening
summary: Paying full attention, paraphrasing, asking clarifying questions and responding with empathy.
---
**Active listening** means fully concentrating on the speaker, understanding them, and showing that you have. It is tested in group discussions, client calls and behavioural interviews.

## Techniques

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| Give full attention | Eye contact, no phone, nodding |
| Paraphrase | "So you're saying the deadline is too tight?" |
| Ask clarifying questions | "When you say urgent, do you mean today?" |
| Reflect feelings | "That sounds frustrating." |
| Don't interrupt | Let them finish before responding |
| Summarise | "To recap, we'll fix the bug first and then test." |

## Barriers

- Planning your reply while the other person is still speaking
- Judging before you've understood
- Distractions and multitasking

=== c-intonation | Intonation
summary: Rising and falling pitch for questions, statements, lists and emotions, and how tone changes meaning.
---
**Intonation** is the rise and fall of your voice. It signals whether you are asking or telling, finishing or continuing, and how you feel.

## Patterns

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Falling ↘** | statements, commands, wh-questions | "I'll send it tomorrow ↘." / "Where do you work ↘?" |
| **Rising ↗** | yes/no questions, uncertainty | "Is the meeting today ↗?" |
| **Rise–fall in lists** | rise on each item, fall on the last | "Bring your ID ↗, resume ↗ and photo ↘." |
| **Fall–rise ↘↗** | politeness, reservation, "but…" | "The idea is good ↘↗… (but)" |

## Why it matters

A flat, monotone voice sounds bored or nervous, even when your words are good. In interviews, falling intonation at the end of statements sounds **confident**; rising at the end of every statement sounds unsure.

Is the meeting today? No, it was moved to Thursday. Please bring your laptop, your notes and the client brief.

=== c-stress-rhythm | Word Stress and Rhythm
summary: Stressed syllables, noun–verb stress pairs, sentence stress on content words and English's stress-timed rhythm.
---
## Word stress

Every English word of two or more syllables has one **stressed syllable**: it is louder, longer and higher in pitch.

| Word | Stress |
|---|---|
| PHOtograph | 1st |
| phoTOgraphy | 2nd |
| photoGRAPHic | 3rd |
| deVELopment | 2nd |

**Noun–verb pairs** shift the stress: a **RE**cord / to re**CORD**, a **PRE**sent / to pre**SENT**, an **OB**ject / to ob**JECT**.

## Sentence stress

Stress the **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives). Reduce the **function words** (a, the, of, to, and):
*I WANT to FINISH the REPORT by FRIDAY.*

Moving the stress changes the meaning:
- *I didn't say HE took it* (someone else did)
- *I didn't SAY he took it* (but I implied it)

## Rhythm

English is **stress-timed**: the stressed syllables come at roughly regular intervals, and the unstressed syllables are squeezed in between. This rhythm is a large part of sounding natural.

:::speak read
I want to finish the report by Friday, so please record the results and present them at the meeting.
:::
