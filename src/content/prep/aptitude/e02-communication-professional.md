=== c-grammar-speech | Grammar in Spoken English
summary: Common spoken-English errors such as "I am having", "discuss about" and "do the needful", and their natural alternatives.
---
Spoken assessments and interviews penalise a small set of **very common errors**. Fix these and you remove most grammar mistakes from your speech.

| ✘ Avoid | ✔ Say |
|---|---|
| I am having two brothers. | I **have** two brothers. (stative verb) |
| We discussed **about** the plan. | We discussed the plan. |
| Please **revert back**. | Please **reply** / get back to me. |
| Myself Ravi. | I'm Ravi. / My name is Ravi. |
| I am **knowing** him for years. | I **have known** him for years. |
| He is **more better**. | He is **better**. |
| One of my **friend**. | One of my **friends**. |
| I **didn't went**. | I **didn't go**. |
| **Do the needful.** | Please **do what's needed** / please **take care of this**. |
| Kindly **adjust**. | Could you please **accommodate this**? |
| What is your **good name**? | What is your name? |

:::tip
Stative verbs (have for possession, know, like, want, believe, understand) usually aren't used in continuous forms.
:::

:::speak free
Introduce yourself in about one minute: your name, education, a project you're proud of, and what you are looking for in your first job.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- I am having two brothers.
- [x] I have two brothers.
- I am have two brothers.
- I having two brothers.
> "Have", in the sense of possession, is stative.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- We discussed about the issue.
- [x] We discussed the issue.
- We discussed on the issue.
- We did discuss about issue.
> "Discuss" takes a direct object.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What is the best way to introduce yourself?
- Myself Ravi.
- [x] I'm Ravi.
- Me Ravi.
- This is myself Ravi.
> "Myself" can't be the subject.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- I am knowing him since college.
- [x] I have known him since college.
- I know him since college.
- I knew him since college.
> A continuing state needs the present perfect.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: What is the natural alternative to "Please revert back at the earliest"?
- Please revert back soon.
- [x] Please get back to me as soon as you can.
- Please revert back fast.
- Please back revert.
> "Revert back" is redundant, and "revert" really means "return to a previous state".
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- I didn't went to the office.
- [x] I didn't go to the office.
- I didn't gone to the office.
- I not went to the office.
> After "did", use the base form.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: What is the clearest professional alternative to "Do the needful"?
- Do needful.
- [x] Please take care of this by Friday.
- Do the necessary needful.
- Kindly do needful things.
> Say exactly what you need done, and by when.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- He is more better at coding.
- [x] He is better at coding.
- He is more good at coding.
- He is most better at coding.
> Avoid double comparatives.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- One of my friend is a doctor.
- [x] One of my friends is a doctor.
- One of my friends are a doctor.
- One my friend is doctor.
> "One of" + plural noun + singular verb.
:::

:::mcq hard
Q: Which sentence sounds the most natural and professional?
- What is your good name, sir?
- Tell your name.
- [x] May I know your name, please?
- Your name is what?
> It is polite and idiomatic.
:::

=== c-vocabulary-communication | Vocabulary for Communication
summary: Choosing precise, polite and professional words, and avoiding vague, informal or overused expressions.
---
Good workplace vocabulary is **precise, polite and plain**, not ornate.

## Upgrade vague words

| Vague | Precise |
|---|---|
| good | effective, reliable, well-structured |
| bad | inefficient, error-prone, unclear |
| a lot of | many, numerous, a significant number of |
| thing | issue, feature, step, requirement |
| get | receive, obtain, understand |

## Polite softeners

- *Could you…?*, *Would you mind…?*, *I was wondering if…*
- *I'm afraid…* (for bad news), *Unfortunately…*
- *I'd suggest…* instead of *You must…*

## Avoid in professional speech

Slang (*gonna*, *wanna*, *cool*), absolute words (*always*, *never*) unless they are true, and jargon your listener may not know.

=== c-topic-speaking | Topic Speaking (JAM)
summary: Structuring a one-minute talk on any topic with the PREP framework, plus openings, examples and conclusions.
---
"Just a Minute" (JAM) and topic-speaking tasks test whether you can **organise thoughts quickly** and speak coherently.

## The PREP framework

1. **P**oint: state your view clearly. *"I believe remote work benefits both companies and employees."*
2. **R**eason: why? *"It removes commuting time and widens the talent pool."*
3. **E**xample: make it concrete. *"My cousin's team hired developers from three cities last year."*
4. **P**oint again: conclude. *"So, used well, remote work is a win for everyone."*

## Tips

- Use the **preparation time** to choose your point and one example; don't script every word.
- **Open strongly.** Never start with "So, umm, the topic is…"
- Two clear reasons beat five vague ones.
- Finish **on time** with a clear concluding line.

:::speak free
Topic: "Should coding be taught in every school?" Prepare for 30 seconds, then speak for one minute using Point, Reason, Example, Point.
:::

:::speak free
Topic: "The most important quality in a team member." Speak for one minute.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What does PREP stand for?
- Plan, Read, Explain, Present
- [x] Point, Reason, Example, Point
- Pause, Repeat, Explain, Pause
- Prepare, Recall, Express, Practise
> It is a simple speaking structure.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which is the strongest way to open a JAM talk?
- "So, umm, my topic is…"
- [x] "I strongly believe that coding should be taught in every school."
- "I don't know much about this."
- "Can I change the topic?"
> A clear position from the first sentence.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: How should you use 30 seconds of preparation time?
- Write a full script
- [x] Choose your point and one strong example
- Stay silent and relax
- Memorise quotes
> Structure matters more than a script.
:::

:::mcq medium

- [x] It makes your reason concrete and convincing
- Examples aren't needed
- To change the topic
> Examples turn claims into evidence.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: How should you end a topic talk?
- Stop abruptly
- [x] Restate your point in one clear sentence

- Say "That's all, I guess"
> It is the final P of PREP.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: Which is better in a one-minute talk?
- Five quick reasons with no detail
- [x] Two clear reasons, each supported
- Only examples, with no point
- Reading out a definition
> Depth beats breadth in a short talk.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: You get a topic you know little about. What should you do?
- Refuse to speak
- [x] Relate it to something you know and keep the structure
- Speak about a different topic
- Speak very fast to finish
> Structure carries you even when your content is thin.

- Giving one example
> These break the flow.
:::

- [x] PREP

:::

> Clarity matters, not taking a side.

=== c-picture-description | Picture Description
summary: Describing a scene in a logical order: overview, foreground and background, people and actions, then interpretation.
---
Picture description (in Versant 4 Skills, Pearson and campus tests) checks vocabulary, fluency and organisation.

## A reliable structure

1. **Overview:** "This picture shows a busy railway station in the morning."
2. **Foreground:** "In the front, a woman is checking her phone near a ticket counter."
3. **Background:** "Behind her, a train is arriving and people are waiting on the platform."
4. **Details:** colours, numbers, expressions, weather.
5. **Interpretation:** "It looks like rush hour; people seem to be in a hurry to get to work."

## Language tools

- Use the **present continuous** for actions: *is walking*, *are talking*.
- **Location phrases:** in the foreground or background, on the left or right, in the centre, next to, behind.
- **Speculation:** *seems to*, *looks like*, *probably*, *might be*.

:::speak free
Describe this scene (imagine it): A small café on a rainy evening. Inside, two friends sit at a window table laughing over coffee. A waiter carries a tray of cakes. Outside, people with umbrellas hurry past the lit window.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which is the best opening for a picture description?
- "There is a man."
- [x] "This picture shows a busy café on a rainy evening."
- "I see colours."
- "The man is wearing blue."
> Start with an overview of the whole scene.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which tense is best for describing actions in a picture?
- Simple past
- [x] Present continuous
- Future perfect
- Past perfect
> "A waiter is carrying a tray."
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: Which phrase describes location in a picture?
- Yesterday
- [x] In the background
- Very quickly
- Because of
> It is a spatial phrase.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: Which phrase is best for speculating about a picture?
- "It is certainly a wedding."
- [x] "It looks like a celebration, probably a birthday."
- "I don't know."
- "Nothing is happening."
> Hedged speculation is accurate and natural.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: What is the best order for a picture description?
- Random details, then an overview
- [x] Overview → foreground → background → details → interpretation
- Interpretation only
- Colours → names → numbers
> From general to specific, then your interpretation.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What should the interpretation part include?
- The size of the photo
- [x] What the scene suggests: mood, time or purpose
- A list of colours
- Your name
> It shows higher-level thinking.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: Which sentence best describes the foreground of the café scene?
- "People exist."
- [x] "In the foreground, two friends are laughing over coffee at a window table."
- "The café is a café."
- "There might be something."
> It gives the location, the subjects and the action.
:::

:::mcq medium
Q: Why use location words such as "on the left" and "behind"?
- To sound formal
- [x] They help the listener picture the layout
- They are required by grammar
- To fill time
> They organise the description spatially.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What is a common mistake in picture description?
- Giving an overview
- [x] Listing objects without saying what is happening
- Using the present continuous
- Speculating politely
> Actions and relationships matter.
:::

:::mcq hard
Q: Describing a busy station, which sentence adds the most value?
- "There are people."

=== c-situation-speaking | Situational Speaking
summary: Responding appropriately to workplace situations such as apologising, requesting, declining and escalating.
---
Situational tasks give you a scenario and ask what you would **say**. The scoring looks at **appropriateness**: tone, politeness, clarity and whether you solve the problem.

## A formula that works

**Acknowledge → explain briefly → offer an action → close politely.**
*"I'm sorry for the delay. The data arrived late, but I'll send the report by 4 pm today. Thank you for your patience."*

## Common situations

- **Apologising:** take responsibility, don't over-explain, give a fix.
- **Requesting:** be polite and specific (what, by when, why).
- **Declining:** appreciate the offer, give a brief reason, offer an alternative if you can.
- **Escalating:** state the facts, the impact and what you need.

:::speak free
Situation: You will be 20 minutes late to a client meeting because of a traffic jam. Call the client and explain.

=== c-professional-communication | Professional Communication
summary: Clarity, conciseness, tone, channel choice and etiquette in workplace communication.
---
## The 7 Cs of communication

**Clear, Concise, Concrete, Correct, Coherent, Complete, Courteous.** Most workplace miscommunication breaks one of these.

## Choosing the right channel

| Situation | Best channel |
|---|---|
| Quick question | chat |
| A decision that needs a written record | email |
| Sensitive or complex feedback | a call or face to face |
| Urgent issue | a call, followed by a written summary |

## Etiquette

- Reply within a working day, even if only to say *"I'll get back to you by Friday."*
- Don't use all capitals (it reads as shouting); use **reply all** only when everyone needs the message.
- Give **feedback** privately and specifically: *the situation → its impact → a suggestion*.
- In meetings: arrive prepared, stay on the agenda, and summarise the actions at the end.

=== c-email-writing | Email Writing
summary: Subject lines, structure, tone, attachments and common email mistakes, for workplace and application emails.
---
## Structure

1. **Subject line:** specific and scannable. *"Request: leave on 12–13 June"*, not *"Hi"*.
2. **Greeting:** *Dear Ms Rao,* (formal) or *Hi Arjun,* (colleague).
3. **Opening line:** the purpose, straight away. *"I'm writing to request…"*
4. **Body:** short paragraphs or bullets with the details and any deadline.
5. **Call to action:** what you need, and by when.
6. **Closing:** *Regards*, *Best regards*, *Thank you*, followed by your name, role and contact details.

## Common mistakes

- Vague subject lines, or none at all
- Burying the request in the third paragraph
- Mentioning an attachment and forgetting to attach it
- Over-familiar tone with people you don't know ("Hey dude")
- Long walls of text with no structure

:::tip
Before sending, ask: could the reader act on this email after reading only the subject line and the first two lines?
:::

=== c-presentation-skills | Presentation Skills
summary: Structuring a talk, designing clear slides, delivery, handling questions and managing nerves.
---
## Structure: tell them, then tell them, then tell them again

1. **Opening:** a hook (a question, a statistic or a short story) and an agenda.
2. **Body:** 3 key points, each with evidence or an example.
3. **Close:** summarise the points and state the action you want.

## Slides

- **One idea per slide**, with a headline that states the point.
- Use few words and large fonts (at least 24 pt); prefer visuals and simple charts.
- Don't read your slides; they support you, they don't replace you.

## Delivery

- Eye contact around the room, an open posture and purposeful gestures
- Varied pitch and pace; pause after key points
- Practise aloud, timed, at least twice

## Questions and answers

**Listen to the whole question → thank the person or repeat the question → answer briefly → check that you've answered it.** If you don't know, say so and offer to follow up.

## Nerves

Prepare the opening thoroughly, breathe slowly before you start, and focus on helping the audience rather than on yourself.

:::speak free
Present for one minute: "Why our team should adopt automated testing." Use an opening hook, three points and a closing action.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What is the best way to open a presentation?
- "Umm, so I'll talk about stuff."
- [x] A hook, such as a surprising statistic or a question, followed by the agenda
- Reading the first slide word for word
- Apologising for being nervous
> A hook earns attention, and the agenda sets expectations.
:::

:::mcq easy
Q: What is a good rule for slide design?
- As much text as possible
- [x] One idea per slide, with few words and large fonts
- Tiny fonts so everything fits
- No headlines
> Simple slides support the speaker.
