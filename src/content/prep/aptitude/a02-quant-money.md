=== q-percentages | Percentages
summary: Percent as a fraction, percent of a number, "what percent" questions, comparisons and exam pass-mark problems.
---
**Percent** means *per hundred*: x% = x/100. Almost every arithmetic topic (profit, interest, DI) is percentages in disguise.

## Core moves

- x% of N = (x/100) × N
- A as a percent of B = (A/B) × 100
- x% of y = y% of x, so 16% of 25 = 25% of 16 = **4**

## Percent → fraction table

| % | Fraction | % | Fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1/10 | 12.5 | 1/8 |
| 20 | 1/5 | 16.67 | 1/6 |
| 25 | 1/4 | 33.33 | 1/3 |
| 50 | 1/2 | 66.67 | 2/3 |
| 75 | 3/4 | 37.5 | 3/8 |

## Comparing two quantities

If A is r% more than B, then B is less than A by **r/(100 + r) × 100 %**.

- A earns 25% more than B, so B earns 25/125 = **20%** less than A.
- If A is r% less than B, then B is more than A by r/(100 − r) × 100 %.

## Keeping spending constant

If a price rises by r%, consumption must fall by r/(100 + r) × 100 % to keep spending the same. For a 25% rise, cut consumption by **20%**.

## Pass/fail questions

**Example.** One candidate scores 30% and fails by 50 marks. Another scores 45% and gets 25 marks more than the pass mark.

The gap between them is 45% − 30% = 15% of the maximum, which equals 50 + 25 = 75 marks.
So the maximum is 75 ÷ 0.15 = **500**, and the pass mark is 150 + 50 = 200.

## Overlapping groups

Pass in both = 100% − (fail A + fail B − fail both).

=== q-percent-change | Percentage Change
summary: Percentage increase and decrease, successive changes and the effect on products such as area and revenue.
---
**Percentage change = (new − old) / old × 100.** The base is always the *old* value. That's why going from 80 to 100 is +25%, while going from 100 to 80 is −20%.

## Successive changes

Two changes of a% and b% (use negatives for decreases) combine to:

**a + b + ab/100 %**

| Changes | Net |
|---|---|
| +10%, +20% | 10 + 20 + 2 = **+32%** |
| +20%, −20% | 20 − 20 − 4 = **−4%** |
| −30%, +30% | **−9%** |

For three or more changes, multiply the factors: +20%, +25%, −10% → 1.2 × 1.25 × 0.9 = 1.35, which is **+35%**.

## Products: area, revenue

When a quantity is a product (area = l × b, revenue = price × quantity), apply the same successive-change rule:

- Length +20%, breadth −10%: area changes by 20 − 10 − 2 = **+8%**
- Side of a square +10%: area changes by 10 + 10 + 1 = **+21%**
- Price +25%, quantity −20%: 1.25 × 0.8 = 1, so revenue is **unchanged**

## Getting back to the start

After a rise of r%, the drop needed to return to the original value is r/(100 + r) × 100 %. After +50%, you need **−33.33%**.

=== q-profit-loss | Profit and Loss
summary: Cost price, selling price, profit and loss percentages, dishonest dealers and chain transactions.
---
- **CP** (cost price) is what you pay; **SP** (selling price) is what you receive.
- Profit = SP − CP; Loss = CP − SP.
- **Profit % and Loss % are always on CP**, unless the question says otherwise.

## Formulas

- SP = CP × (100 + P%)/100
- SP = CP × (100 − L%)/100
- CP = SP × 100/(100 + P%)

**Example.** SP ₹660 at 10% profit: CP = 660 × 100/110 = **₹600**.

## Classic patterns

| Pattern | Result |
|---|---|
| Two items sold at the same SP, one at x% profit and one at x% loss | Always a **loss of x²/100 %** |
| CP of a items = SP of b items | Profit % = (a − b)/b × 100 |
| Dealer uses a false weight (gives w g for 1000 g) and sells at CP | Profit % = (1000 − w)/w × 100 |
| Profit at SP₁ equals loss at SP₂ | CP = (SP₁ + SP₂)/2 |

**Example.** A dealer gives 900 g for every kilogram. Profit = 100/900 × 100 = **11.11%**.

## Chain transactions

A sells to B at 10% profit, and B sells to C at 20% profit. C pays ₹264.
A's cost = 264 ÷ (1.1 × 1.2) = **₹200**.

## Buying and selling in bundles

Buy at 6 for ₹10 and sell at 4 for ₹10. Make the quantities equal (12 items): CP = ₹20, SP = ₹30, so the profit is **50%**.

=== q-discounts | Discounts and Marked Price
summary: Marked price, discount, successive discounts, buy-x-get-y offers and marking up to hit a target profit.
---
The **marked price (MP)** is the list price. A **discount is always on MP**, while profit is on CP.

- SP = MP × (100 − d%)/100
- Discount % = (MP − SP)/MP × 100

## Successive discounts

Discounts of a% and b% equal a single discount of **a + b − ab/100 %**. For 20% then 10%: 20 + 10 − 2 = **28%** (not 30%).

For three discounts, multiply the remaining fractions: 10%, 20% and 25% leave 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.75 = 0.54 of the price, a **46%** discount.

## Mark-up and discount together

If the MP is m% above CP and a discount of d% is given:

**SP/CP = (1 + m/100)(1 − d/100)**

- Mark up 25%, discount 10%: 1.25 × 0.9 = 1.125, a **12.5% profit**.
- To earn 20% profit after a 25% discount: MP/CP = 1.2/0.75 = 1.6, so mark up **60%**.

## Offers

"Buy x get y free" gives a discount of y/(x + y) × 100 %. "Buy 3 get 1 free" is 1/4 = **25%**.

=== q-simple-interest | Simple Interest
summary: SI = PRT/100, finding any one of principal, rate or time, doubling and tripling, and split investments.
---
**Simple interest** is charged only on the original principal:

**SI = P × R × T / 100**, and **Amount A = P + SI**.

## Rearranged forms

- P = 100 × SI / (R × T)
- R = 100 × SI / (P × T)
- T = 100 × SI / (P × R)

## Multiplying a sum

To become n times itself, a sum must earn SI of (n − 1)P, so **R × T = 100(n − 1)**.

- Doubles in 8 years: R = 100/8 = **12.5%**
- Triples at 10%: T = 200/10 = **20 years**

## Two amounts at two times

If a sum becomes A₁ after T₁ years and A₂ after T₂ years:
- SI per year = (A₂ − A₁)/(T₂ − T₁)
- P = A₁ − T₁ × (SI per year)

**Example.** ₹1240 after 3 years and ₹1400 after 5 years. SI per year = 160/2 = 80, so P = 1240 − 240 = **₹1000** and R = **8%**.

## Split investments

**Example.** ₹10,000 is lent partly at 8% and the rest at 10%, earning ₹920 a year in total.
0.08x + 0.10(10,000 − x) = 920, so 1000 − 0.02x = 920 and x = **₹4000** at 8%.

=== q-compound-interest | Compound Interest
summary: Interest on interest, half-yearly compounding, CI vs SI difference, growth and depreciation.
---
With **compound interest**, each period's interest is added to the principal, so the next period earns interest on it too.

**A = P(1 + R/100)^n**, and **CI = A − P**.

## Compounding more often

If compounding happens k times a year, use rate R/k and nk periods.
- ₹10,000 at 20% a year compounded half-yearly for 1 year = 10,000 × 1.1² = **₹12,100**
- The effective annual rate of 10% compounded half-yearly is 1.05² − 1 = **10.25%**

## Difference between CI and SI

| Years | CI − SI |
|---|---|
| 2 | P(R/100)² |
| 3 | P(R/100)²(3 + R/100) |

**Example.** ₹5000 at 10% for 2 years: 5000 × 0.01 = **₹50**.

**Reverse it.** CI for 2 years is ₹410 and SI is ₹400. SI per year = 200, and the extra ₹10 is interest on ₹200 for one year, so R = **5%** and P = **₹4000**.

## Consecutive amounts

If the amount is A₁ after n years and A₂ after n + 1 years, the rate is A₂/A₁ − 1.
₹6050 → ₹6655: the ratio is 1.1, so R = **10%** and P = 6050/1.21 = **₹5000**.

## Multiplying sums

At compound interest, if a sum doubles in t years it becomes 4× in 2t years and 8× in 3t years.

## Growth and depreciation

The same formula works for populations and asset values. Use (1 − R/100) for a decrease:
50,000 falling 10% a year for 2 years → 50,000 × 0.81 = **40,500**.
